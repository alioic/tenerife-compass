Anaga: laurel forests, hairpins & wild beaches

Half an hour from the capital, the road climbs into a forest that was already old before the ice ages, then drops down hairpins to black-sand beaches where the Atlantic arrives with real force. Anaga is the part of Tenerife that looks nothing like the brochures, and it rewards a full, unhurried day.

What Anaga actually is

The north-eastern corner of Tenerife is the oldest part of the island. The rock under your boots here cooled roughly seven to nine million years ago, long before the volcano that became Teide even existed, and those millions of years of erosion are what carved the landscape you see now: knife-edge ridges, ravines that plunge from 900 metres to the sea in a few kilometres, and tiny hamlets sitting at the end of dead-end roads.

The whole massif is a UNESCO Biosphere Reserve, and the reason is the forest. The high ridge is covered in laurisilva — subtropical laurel forest of the kind that blanketed much of southern Europe and North Africa before the ice ages wiped it out almost everywhere. It survived here because the trade-wind clouds snag on the ridge and keep it permanently damp. Walking through it feels less like Spain and more like something prehistoric: moss on everything, twisted trunks, ferns at head height, and cloud drifting between the trees. Anaga holds one of the highest concentrations of endemic species anywhere in Europe, and you do not need to be a botanist to feel that the place is different.

Getting oriented

The gateway is the Cruz del Carmen visitor centre, up on the ridge at about 920 metres where the TF-12 road crosses the crest. Start here on your first visit: the staff hand out trail maps, answer permit questions, and the mirador behind the centre looks back over La Laguna to Teide on a clear day. Several of the best walks begin in the car park.

Two roads matter. The TF-12 runs along the spine of the massif from La Laguna towards San Andrés, mostly in or just below the cloud forest. The TF-134 drops off it at El Bailadero and winds down to Taganana and the north coast beaches. Distances are deceptive: nothing in Anaga is far on the map, but everything is a hairpin road, so a 30-kilometre drive can honestly take an hour. Treat the driving as part of the day rather than the cost of it.

The hikes

Anaga has dozens of marked trails. These six cover the range, from a pushchair-friendly loop to a full ridge-to-sea descent.

Sentidos

Sendero de los Sentidos

Three short "path of the senses" loops from Cruz del Carmen, partly on boardwalk under the laurels. The gentlest possible introduction to the forest, and genuinely good with kids.

Punta del Hidalgo

Cruz del Carmen → Punta del Hidalgo

The classic ridge-to-sea descent (PR-TF 10): cloud forest, then open ridgeline, then the coast, passing Chinamada on the way. One-way, so plan a bus or taxi back up.

Afur

Afur → Taganana coast

Down the Afur ravine past palm trees and an actual stream — rare on Tenerife — to the wild beach at Tamadite, then along the cliffs to Taganana. Rough underfoot in places.

El Pijaral

Bosque Encantado (El Pijaral)

The "enchanted forest" loop from La Ensillada: the densest, mossiest laurisilva in the park. Beautiful, protected, and it requires a free advance permit — details below.

Chinamada

Chinamada & the cave houses

A hamlet where people still live in houses dug into the rock, with whitewashed fronts. Walk in from Cruz del Carmen or up from Punta del Hidalgo; the Aguaide viewpoint is the payoff.

El Draguillo

Benijo → El Draguillo

An easy out-and-back along the coast beyond the end of the tarmac, with the Roques de Anaga sea stacks offshore the whole way. Ideal if you want views without a big climb.

The Bosque Encantado permit

El Pijaral is an integral nature reserve, so access is capped — at the time of writing, 45 people per day — and you need a free permit booked in advance. There is no ticket office at the trailhead and no way to talk your way in: wardens do check, and they turn people away.

You book through the Cabildo de Tenerife's reservation portal, Tenerife ON (centralreservas.tenerife.es, also an app). Create a free account first, because slots go quickly: new dates are released on a rolling window of roughly two weeks, and weekends can be gone within minutes of opening. One booking covers up to five people. On the day, carry the permit on your phone along with the ID you booked with. If you cannot get a slot, do not force it — the Sendero de los Sentidos and the forest around Cruz del Carmen give you the same mossy laurisilva atmosphere with no paperwork at all.

Taganana and the beaches

When the TF-134 finally uncoils at the bottom, you arrive in Taganana, a scatter of white houses and terraced vineyards founded in the 1500s and still feeling like it. From there the road runs along the coast through Roque de las Bodegas and Almáciga before the tarmac gives up entirely just past Playa de Benijo.

These are black-sand beaches with serious Atlantic surf, and they need to be treated honestly. There are no lifeguards to speak of, the shore break is heavy, and currents here catch out strong swimmers every year. Locals surf; visitors should paddle with great caution and stay out entirely when the sea is up. What the beaches lack in safe swimming they repay in scenery: Benijo at low tide, with the Roques de Anaga sea stacks black against a red sky, is probably the best sunset photograph on the island. The steps down from the road are steep — count on a sweaty walk back up.

The honest practicalities

Expect mist. It can be blazing sunshine in Santa Cruz and drizzle on the ridge twenty minutes later — that is not bad luck, it is the trade-wind cloud that keeps the forest alive. Bring layers and shoes with real grip, because the trails are muddy and root-laced even in summer. The coast below is often sunny while the crest sits in cloud, so a mixed forecast rarely ruins the day.

The driving is slow. Santa Cruz to Benijo is only about 35 kilometres but takes close to an hour of continuous hairpins, and the coastal stretch is narrow enough that you will be reversing for oncoming cars occasionally. At weekends, parking fills early at Cruz del Carmen and above Benijo — arrive before 10:00 or come on a weekday.

Without a car it is hard. TITSA buses 946 and 947 do connect La Laguna with Taganana and the ridge, but departures are sparse and do not line up well with one-way hikes. Realistically, rent a car for the day or take a guided tour. For city logistics, start with our Santa Cruz & La Laguna guide, or see the rest of the island on the region map.

If you'd rather be guided

A good guide solves the two genuine problems with Anaga: the driving and the one-way trail logistics. They also know which side of the ridge the sun is on that day, which matters more here than almost anywhere on the island. Half-day laurel-forest hikes typically run €40–60 per person with hotel pickup; simpler minibus sightseeing loops start around €25, and small-group full days with a proper hike and a Taganana lunch stop reach €95.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need a permit to hike in Anaga?

Only for one trail: the Bosque Encantado / El Pijaral loop, which needs a free advance permit from the Cabildo's Tenerife ON portal (centralreservas.tenerife.es), capped at 45 people a day. Every other trail in this guide, including Cruz del Carmen to Punta del Hidalgo and the Afur–Taganana route, is free and open with no booking.

Can beginners hike in Anaga?

Yes, if they pick the right trail. The Sendero de los Sentidos loops and the Benijo–El Draguillo coast path are easy and well marked. The longer routes involve steep, sometimes slippery descents, so save those until you know how the terrain feels. Grippy shoes matter more than fitness on the short trails.

Can you swim at Playa de Benijo?

With great caution, and sometimes not at all. It is an open Atlantic beach with heavy shore break and strong currents, and there is no lifeguard service you can rely on. On calm days locals do swim near the shore; if the surf is up, stay on the sand and enjoy the view instead.

How do I get to Anaga without a car?

TITSA buses 946 and 947 run from La Laguna towards Taganana and the ridge villages, but services are sparse and awkward for hikers, especially for one-way routes. A hire car or a guided tour with hotel pickup is the realistic answer for most visitors.

When is the weather best in Anaga?

There is no reliably dry season on the ridge — the trade-wind mist is what keeps the forest alive, and it can appear any month. Mornings are generally clearer than afternoons, and the coast at Taganana and Benijo is often sunny when the crest is in cloud. Bring layers whenever you come.
