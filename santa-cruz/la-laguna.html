La Laguna: the island's UNESCO old town

Five centuries of painted mansions, carved wooden balconies and hidden courtyards — with a university crowd keeping the tapas bars loud until midnight. This is the Tenerife most beach visitors never see.

Home› Santa Cruz & La Laguna› La Laguna

Why La Laguna matters

San Cristóbal de La Laguna was Tenerife's first capital, founded in 1496 just after the Spanish conquest, and for two centuries it was the most important town in the Canaries. What makes it genuinely special — and earned it UNESCO World Heritage status in 1999 — is the street plan. La Laguna was the first Spanish colonial town laid out on an unfortified grid, drawn up from scratch rather than growing around a castle. That grid became the template exported across the Atlantic: walk these streets and you're walking the prototype for Old Havana, Cartagena and dozens of other colonial cities in the Americas. The historic core is still essentially intact, with hundreds of buildings from the 16th to 18th centuries.

Here's the thing that keeps it from feeling like an open-air museum: La Laguna is a working university town. The Universidad de La Laguna has tens of thousands of students, so between the convents and the mansions you get cheap lunch menus, secondhand bookshops, skateboarders in the plazas and bars that actually fill up on a Tuesday. It's a place people live in, not a place preserved in amber — and that's exactly why it's worth half a day of anyone's trip.

What to see in the old town

Everything below sits within a ten-minute walk of everything else. You don't need a plan so much as a loose list — but these are the stops that reward you most.

Iglesia-Catedral

The Cathedral

La Laguna's cathedral hides a surprise: behind the neoclassical facade is a 20th-century rebuild with one of Europe's early large concrete domes. Inside are the remains of Alonso Fernández de Lugo, the conquistador who founded the town. Free to poke your head in during services; a small fee otherwise.

Concepción tower

Iglesia de la Concepción

The town's oldest parish church, and the one thing you should pay for. Climb the bell tower — around 100 steps, roughly €2 — for the best view in town: terracotta rooftops in a perfect grid, with the Anaga mountains behind. Go on a clear morning. The bells do ring; you've been warned.

Plaza del Adelantado

Plaza del Adelantado

The old power centre: town hall, the Santa Catalina convent with its shuttered wooden gallery (nuns once watched the square through it unseen), and a leafy square where locals actually sit. The small market building nearby is good for a coffee stop.

Mansion streets

Obispo Rey Redondo & San Agustín

The two showpiece streets. Calle San Agustín in particular is lined with 16th–18th-century mansions in ochre, oxblood and blue. Many have open doors — step into the courtyards. Casa Salazar and Casa Montañés are the ones to look for, all dark Canarian pine balconies around a patio.

Casa Lercaro

Casa Lercaro

A Genoese merchant's mansion that now houses the Museum of History and Anthropology of Tenerife. If you want the story behind everything you're looking at — conquest, sugar, wine, pirates — this is an hour well spent, and the building itself is one of the best on the street.

Teatro Leal

Teatro Leal

A flamboyant 1915 theatre on Obispo Rey Redondo, all pink and gold on the outside, restored beautifully inside. Check the programme — catching a concert here is a lovely way to end a day in town, and tickets are usually cheap.

The tapas scene

La Laguna eats better than a town this size has any right to, mostly because students keep prices honest and locals keep standards high. Three places we'd send a friend to:

Taberna La Casa de Óscar (€€) — pintxos and grilled meat in the former house of surrealist painter Óscar Domínguez, La Laguna's most famous son. It's noisy, fast and genuinely good, which is why it's packed at weekends; go early or be ready to hover at the bar. Guaydil (€€€) — the creative end of Canarian cooking, local produce treated seriously without tipping into fussiness. This is the reservation-worthy one; book a few days ahead. Tasca 61 (€€) — tiny, owner-run, with a short seasonal blackboard menu that changes with what came in that morning. The kind of place you leave planning your return visit.

Beyond those, just follow the pedestrian streets around Calle Herradores at lunch: the menú del día boards aimed at students are some of the best-value cooked meals on the island. Order papas arrugadas with both mojos at least once — it's practically the law up here.

A stroll route (2–3 hours)

Start at the Trinidad tram stop at the top of town and walk down Calle Obispo Rey Redondo, the old "Calle Real". You'll pass the Teatro Leal and the cathedral before reaching Iglesia de la Concepción — climb the tower first, while the morning light is good and the queue is short. From there, double back along Calle San Agustín, taking your time with the mansion courtyards and stopping at Casa Lercaro if the history has hooked you. Cut down to Plaza del Adelantado for the convent and a coffee, then let yourself get slightly lost in the smaller grid streets — Herradores, Carrera — which is where the bakeries and tapas bars live. Finish with lunch somewhere you spotted along the way. If it's a Sunday, note that some churches close to visitors around services, but the streets themselves are at their most local.

Practical: getting there and what to expect

The tram is the easy answer. Line 1 runs from central Santa Cruz up to La Laguna every few minutes, takes about 30–40 minutes depending on where you board, and a single costs €1.35. Get off at Trinidad for the old town. It's honestly one of the more pleasant public-transport rides on the island — you climb 550 metres without noticing.

Bring a layer. That 550 metres matters. La Laguna sits on a high plateau and is reliably cooler, cloudier and occasionally drizzlier than the coast — locals call it "the fridge of Tenerife", only half joking. On a 27°C beach day it can be 19°C and misty up here. A light jacket solves everything, and the cool air is half the charm.

Driving? The old town is largely pedestrianised, so don't try to be clever. Park in one of the car parks on the edge (the one near Plaza San Cristóbal works well) and walk in — nothing is more than ten minutes away.

Make a day of it. La Laguna is the gateway to the Anaga mountains, and the two pair perfectly: old town in the morning, laurel forest in the afternoon. See our Anaga guide for routes.

La Laguna FAQ

Is La Laguna worth visiting?

Yes — it's the best-preserved historic town in the Canaries and one of only a handful of UNESCO World Heritage sites in the islands. If you have any interest in history, architecture or just eating well in handsome surroundings, it's the strongest half-day trip from Santa Cruz or the north.

How long do you need?

Half a day covers the old town comfortably: two to three hours of strolling plus lunch. Add the Casa Lercaro museum and a tower climb and you're at a relaxed four to five hours. Combining it with Anaga fills a full day nicely.

How do I get there from Santa Cruz or the south?

From Santa Cruz, take tram Line 1 (about 30–40 minutes, €1.35 single, get off at Trinidad). From the south, drive the TF-1 and TF-2 (about 50–60 minutes to the edge-of-town car parks) or take a Titsa bus to Santa Cruz and switch to the tram.

Why is La Laguna UNESCO-listed?

It was the first unfortified Spanish colonial town built on a planned grid, and that 15th–16th-century layout became the model for colonial cities across the Americas. The historic core survives almost unchanged, with hundreds of protected buildings.

Is it cold in La Laguna?

Cold is a stretch, but it's noticeably cooler than the coast — the town sits at around 550 metres and catches cloud off the trade winds. Expect temperatures several degrees below the beach and pack a light jacket, especially from November to March or in the evening.
