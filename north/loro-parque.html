Loro Parque: the complete guide

Tenerife's most famous attraction is also its most debated. Here's what it costs, what you'll see, and the honest conversation about the orcas — so you can decide for yourself.

Home› North Tenerife› Loro Parque

What is Loro Parque?

Loro Parque sits at Punta Brava, on the western edge of Puerto de la Cruz in north Tenerife. It opened in 1972 as a parrot park — "loro" is Spanish for parrot — with 150 birds and a modest banana plantation for grounds. Fifty years on, it holds one of the largest parrot collections on earth, alongside gorillas, tigers, penguins, sloths, an aquarium, and the marine mammals that make it both wildly popular and genuinely controversial.

On pure visitor experience, it delivers. TripAdvisor has repeatedly rated it among the best zoos in the world, and it shows in the details: the landscaping is lush and mature, the enclosures are generally spacious by zoo standards, and the whole park feels cared for rather than corporate. Most visitors spend a full day here and still miss things.

That said, this is not a neutral attraction. Loro Parque keeps orcas and dolphins in performing shows, which a growing number of travellers are not comfortable with. We cover that fully below, because we think you should walk in — or choose not to — with your eyes open.

The highlights

The park is compact enough to walk comfortably but dense enough to fill a day. These are the bits people remember.

Penguin Planet

One of the biggest indoor penguin exhibits in the world: a genuinely cold, snow-dusted dome where king and rockhopper penguins waddle a few metres from your face. A highlight for almost everyone, and blissfully cool on a hot day.

The parrots

The park's founding purpose and still its soul. Hundreds of species, from hyacinth macaws to tiny lorikeets, many bred here as part of conservation programmes. Easy to rush past; don't.

Katandra Treetops

A walk-through aviary where Australian birds fly free around you. Loud, chaotic, and one of the most fun ten minutes in the park, especially with kids.

Gorillas and big cats

A bachelor group of western lowland gorillas in a large open enclosure, plus lions, tigers and jaguars. The gorilla habitat is among the better ones you'll see in a European zoo.

Aquarium and shark tunnel

A solid mid-size aquarium with a walk-through tunnel, rays, sharks and a hypnotic jellyfish gallery. Good rainy-hour insurance, not that Puerto gets much rain in summer.

Orca, dolphin and sea lion shows

The park's biggest draws and its biggest controversy. Technically impressive, undeniably popular — and the reason many travellers choose to skip Loro Parque entirely. Read the next section before you decide.

The welfare debate, honestly

Loro Parque keeps orcas — originally on loan from SeaWorld — and performs daily shows with them, along with dolphins and sea lions. Since the 2013 documentary Blackfish put captive orcas under a global spotlight, the park has faced sustained criticism from animal-welfare organisations. The core argument against is simple: orcas are large, intelligent, wide-ranging social animals, and critics say no tank, however well built, can meet their needs. Several orcas have died at the park over the years, each death renewing the scrutiny. SeaWorld ended its own orca breeding programme in 2016; Loro Parque has continued to keep and display its whales.

The park's answer is its conservation arm, Loro Parque Fundación, which has channelled well over $25 million into field projects since 1994 and is credited with helping save several parrot species from extinction — work that even some critics acknowledge is real and significant. The park argues its animals are ambassadors that fund and inspire that conservation, that its facilities meet or exceed European zoo standards, and that its animals receive veterinary care most wild animals never could. Independent zoo associations have accredited it; welfare campaigners dispute what that accreditation is worth for cetaceans specifically.

Where does that leave you? Both sides are arguing in good faith about a genuinely hard question, and we're not going to pretend there's a tidy answer.

Our take: this is a personal call, and plenty of thoughtful people land on both sides. If performing cetaceans sit wrong with you, skip the park — the parrot collection alone doesn't justify the ticket, and Tenerife offers a superb alternative: seeing pilot whales and dolphins wild and free on a whale-watching trip from the south coast. If you do go, you'll find the rest of the park excellent and the foundation's parrot work legitimate. Either way, decide before you're standing at the gate with kids asking about the orca show.

Getting there

First things first: Loro Parque is in the north, in Puerto de la Cruz — not near the southern resorts, despite what some excursion sellers imply. From Los Cristianos, Costa Adeje or Playa de las Américas it's roughly a 1-hour to 1-hour-15 drive up the TF-1 and TF-5 motorways. The drive itself is easy and mostly motorway.

Your options:

  • Hire car: the most flexible. Official parking is about €7, and you can pair the visit with La Orotava or the north coast.
  • Coach transfer ticket: entry bundled with return coach from the southern resorts, typically €55–65 per adult. Zero hassle, but you're tied to the coach schedule.
  • Public bus: Titsa runs south-to-Puerto services with a change; doable but slow. Fine if you're already staying in Puerto de la Cruz.
  • The free road train: a little tourist train has traditionally shuttled between central Puerto de la Cruz (Playa Martiánez area) and the park gates for free. Check locally that it's still running, as the service has come and gone over the years.

Practical tips

  • Give it a full day. The park opens around 09:30 and closes at 17:30 (last entry usually 16:00). Arriving at opening genuinely matters — the first two hours are the quietest.
  • Grab a show schedule at the gate and plan your loop around it if you're attending shows; the venues are at opposite ends of the park.
  • Eat early or late. The restaurants jam up between 13:00 and 14:30. There's a decent self-service option, or bring your own picnic.
  • Puerto's microclimate is milder than the south — bring a light layer even in summer, and don't cancel over morning cloud; it often burns off.
  • Twin Ticket maths: if Siam Park is already on your list, the combined ticket saves a meaningful chunk over buying separately.

So — is it worth it?

Verdict: as a day out, Loro Parque is one of the best-executed animal parks in Europe — beautiful grounds, world-class parrot and penguin exhibits, and easily a full day of things to see. Families and animal lovers who are comfortable with cetacean shows will have a great time and get fair value at ~€44. If keeping orcas in shows crosses a line for you — and for many travellers it now does — skip it without regret and put the money towards watching whales in the open Atlantic instead. Tenerife is one of the few places in Europe where you can see them wild, year-round, an hour from your hotel.

Frequently asked questions

How much are Loro Parque tickets in 2026?

Standard entry starts at around €44 for adults and €32 for children aged 3–11, with under-3s free. The Twin Ticket combining Loro Parque and Siam Park starts at roughly €74 for adults and is the best value if you're visiting both. Prices change, so check current rates before booking.

How long do you need at Loro Parque?

Plan a full day. The park opens around 09:30 and closes at 17:30, and between the exhibits and show times, most visitors use every hour. Arriving at opening beats the coach-tour crowds by about ninety minutes.

How far is Loro Parque from the south of Tenerife?

It's in Puerto de la Cruz on the north coast, roughly a 1-hour to 1-hour-15 drive from Costa Adeje, Playa de las Américas or Los Cristianos. Tickets with return coach transfer from the southern resorts cost about €55–65 per adult.

Does Loro Parque still have orca shows?

Yes. The park continues to keep orcas and holds daily presentations with them, along with dolphin and sea lion shows. This is the source of an ongoing animal-welfare debate: critics argue orcas shouldn't be kept in tanks, while the park points to its accreditations and the conservation work of Loro Parque Fundación. Some travellers choose to skip the park on these grounds and see whales in the wild instead.

Is Loro Parque good for young children?

Very. Paths are pram-friendly, Katandra Treetops and Penguin Planet are big hits with small kids, and there are baby-changing facilities and plenty of shade. The full day can be tiring for toddlers, so build in a lunch break.
