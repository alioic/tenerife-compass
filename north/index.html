The other Tenerife

Why the north feels like a different island

Tourism in Tenerife started here, decades before the south existed as anything but fishing villages and banana plantations. Puerto de la Cruz was hosting European visitors in the 1880s, and it shows: real streets, real plazas, and locals who outnumber tourists in most bars. The trade-off is the weather, and we would rather you knew about it before booking.

Weather honesty

The north is green for a reason: it is cloudier. Trade winds push moist air against the mountains, forming a cloud layer locals call the panza de burro (donkey's belly) that can sit over the coast for days, especially in summer. Expect temperatures 2–4°C cooler than the south, and swap guaranteed sunshine for lush ravines and dramatic light. If lying by a pool in reliable sun is the whole point of your trip, stay south and visit the north on a day out. And note the sea: the Atlantic here has real swell, which is exactly why the towns built seawater lidos instead of relying on their beaches.

Puerto de la Cruz

The north's main resort town, and still a proper town underneath. Faded grand hotels, a working fishing harbour, the Lago Martiánez lido and the best-value restaurant scene on the island.

Base for most visitors

La Orotava

A hillside town of carved wooden balconies, cobbled lanes and old-money mansions above the valley that shares its name. Come for the architecture, stay for the oldest patisserie in the Canaries.

Prettiest old town

Icod de los Vinos

A wine town famous for one very old resident: the Drago Milenario, a dragon tree estimated at around 800 years. The town itself is low-key and mostly done in an hour or two.

Quick stop

Garachico

Once Tenerife's richest port, buried by a volcanic eruption in 1706 and rebuilt with quiet dignity. Cobbled squares, two lovely small hotels, and lava rock pools where the harbour used to be.

Most atmospheric

One more name worth knowing: Los Realejos, between Puerto and Icod. Tour buses skip it, but it has the Rambla de Castro coastal walk on its doorstep and a dense cluster of guachinches, the semi-legal home restaurants that make north Tenerife such a good place to be hungry.

Things to do

Lidos, gardens and one very old tree

The north does not really do organised fun. Its best days are made of a morning swim, a long lunch and a slow walk through somewhere old.

Loro Parque

Tenerife's most famous zoo: penguins, gorillas, an enormous parrot collection and polished shows. It fills a full day. Honest note: it keeps orcas and dolphins in performing shows, and many travellers now avoid it on animal-welfare grounds. We would rather lay that out and let you decide.

~€44 adult · €32 child · twin ticket with Siam Park ~€70

Check tickets

Lago Martiánez

César Manrique's 1977 seawater lido: pools, fountains and sculpted volcanic rock filling the seafront where the Atlantic is too rough to swim. Sunbeds, bars and a slightly retro glamour. This is how Puerto swims, and it works.

~€5.50 entry · Puerto de la Cruz

Jardín Botánico

Founded in 1788 to acclimatise plants from the Spanish colonies before they travelled on to the mainland. A compact, shady hour among enormous figs and flowering trees, and possibly the island's best €3.

~€3 Puerto de la Cruz

Playa Jardín

Puerto's main beach: black volcanic sand backed by Manrique-designed gardens, with Teide looming behind on clear days. The swell is real, so respect the flags; on rough days it is a beach for walking, not swimming.

Free Puerto de la Cruz

Rambla de Castro walk

An easy coastal path through palm groves, past an old water-pumping station perched over the ocean and a small hermitage. About 5 km out-and-back from Mirador San Pedro in Los Realejos. Go late afternoon for the light.

Free Los Realejos

Drago Milenario, Icod

The most famous dragon tree in the world, thought to be around 800 years old. The paid park gets you close; the free view from the church square above is genuinely good, and for many people it is enough.

€5 park entry · free from the square

El Caletón rock pools

When the 1706 eruption destroyed Garachico's harbour, it left this lattice of lava pools instead. Swimming here on a calm summer day is one of the island's finest free pleasures. Realistically calm from about May to October; closed in swell.

Free Garachico

La Orotava old town

Steep streets of 16th- and 17th-century houses, churches and formal gardens. The Casas de los Balcones, with their famous carved teak balconies, are the set piece; wandering without a plan is the actual attraction.

Free Casas de los Balcones ~€5–7

Teide from the north

The TF-21 climbs from La Orotava through pine forest into the crater, and it is the most scenic approach to the volcano. Tours pick up in Puerto de la Cruz; self-driving gives you the sunrise miradors to yourself.

From ~€40–90 guided tours · full Teide guide

About "whale watching from Puerto de la Cruz"

Tours sold from Puerto (~€65) include a bus transfer to the south-west coast, because that is where the resident whales and the boats actually are. No whale-watching fleet sails from Puerto itself. Nothing wrong with the trips, but you are buying a coach ride plus a boat, so factor in the extra hours.

Where to stay

Grand old hotels at friendly prices

Northern hotels are older, greener and noticeably cheaper than their southern equivalents. Puerto de la Cruz has the choice; Garachico has the romance.

Hotel Botánico & The Oriental Spa Garden

The Canaries' only hotel classified 5-star Gran Lujo, set in subtropical gardens above Puerto with a serious Thai-inspired spa. Old-school luxury rather than beach-club flash.

From ~€250 / night · 5★ GL · Puerto de la Cruz

Hotel Tigaiga

Family-run since 1959, up in the quiet Taoro Park with big valley views and a loyal repeat clientele. The kind of hotel where staff remember your name by breakfast.

Food & drink

Where the island actually eats well

The north is Tenerife's larder: wine country, banana country, and home of the guachinche. Prices run well below the southern resorts for noticeably better cooking.

El Taller Seve Díaz €€€

Puerto's small, serious tasting-menu restaurant, awarded its first Michelin star in the 2026 guide. Modern Canarian cooking in a handful of covers; book far ahead.

Restaurante Régulo €€

Classic fish and seafood served across the floors of an 18th-century courtyard house near the harbour. A Puerto institution for good reason.

Tito's Bodeguita €€

Grilled meat and fish in a rambling garden with sea views. Relaxed, generous and best in the golden hour before sunset.

Brunelli's Steakhouse €€€

Dry-aged steaks cooked at ferocious temperatures, with the Atlantic crashing outside the windows at Punta Brava. The north's splurge for meat eaters.

Casa Egon €

The oldest patisserie in the Canaries, serving La Orotava since 1916. Coffee, cakes and a tiled interior that has barely changed. Non-negotiable stop in town.

Ardeola Arrocería €€

Rice dishes and local fish beside the El Caletón pools in Garachico. Order the arroz, take the long lunch the setting demands.

Guachinche Casa Pedro €

An award-winning, modern take on the guachinche in Los Realejos: farmhouse portions and house wine at prices that seem like a misprint.

Guachinche El Romance €

The traditional article in La Orotava's hills: their own wine, a short blackboard of home cooking, plastic chairs, no ceremony. Note that guachinches open seasonally and close when the year's wine runs out, so check before driving up.

Parking: Puerto de la Cruz is easiest via its underground car parks rather than hunting street spaces; Garachico has free lots along the seafront. La Orotava's old town is steep and tight, so park low and walk up.

How long do you need?

A day trip from the south touches Puerto, the dragon tree and Garachico but rushes all three. Two to four nights lets the north do what it does best: slow mornings, lido swims, long lunches and towns that feel different once the coaches leave.

FAQ

North Tenerife questions, answered straight

Is North Tenerife worth visiting?

Yes, especially if you care about towns, food and landscape more than guaranteed sunshine. It has the island's best old towns, its cheapest good restaurants and its greenest scenery. Sun-first beach holidays are still better served in the south.

Is the north always cloudy?

No, but it is cloudier than the south. The trade-wind cloud layer comes and goes, sometimes burning off by afternoon, sometimes sitting for days, and summer mornings are often grey. Winter frequently brings clear, mild days. Plan for mixed weather and treat full sun as a bonus.

Is Loro Parque ethical? Is it worth it?

As a day out it is polished and genuinely full; as an institution it keeps orcas and dolphins performing in shows, which a growing number of visitors are not comfortable funding. We do not make that call for you. If marine mammal shows sit badly with you, skip it and spend the day in Garachico instead.

Can you swim in the north?

Yes, but usually in managed spots rather than open surf. The Atlantic here has real swell, which is why Puerto built the Lago Martiánez lido and Garachico uses its natural lava pools. Playa Jardín is swimmable on calm days with lifeguards and flags; always respect a red flag.

Can I do the north as a day trip from the south?

Yes. It is about 75 minutes each way by motorway, and a loop of Puerto de la Cruz, Icod's dragon tree and Garachico makes a full, slightly rushed day. If the north is what drew you to Tenerife in the first place, stay two or more nights instead.
