Los Gigantes & Masca

A wall of cliffs dropping straight into the Atlantic and a village that clings to a ridge above a deep gorge. This is the west coast, and it is one of the easiest big days out you can have from the resorts in the south.

The Giants, up close

Los Gigantes means "the giants", and the name is not marketing. These are sheer basalt cliffs that rise almost vertically out of the sea, in places close to 600 metres high. From the little town that shares their name you get a good side-on view, especially from the harbour wall and the black-sand beach at Playa de los Guios. But the honest truth is that from land you never quite feel the scale. The cliffs are best seen the way the fishermen have always seen them, from the water looking up.

The marina at Los Gigantes is small and pretty, packed with pleasure boats and a handful of restaurants where you can sit with a beer and watch the cliffs change colour as the afternoon light shifts. It is a nice enough place to potter for an hour, but most people come here to get on a boat.

Seeing the cliffs from the water

This is the reason to make the trip. A boat ride along the base of the cliffs is genuinely one of the best two or three hours you can spend in Tenerife, and there is something for every budget and comfort level. Bigger catamarans run half-day cruises that sail past the cliffs, anchor in a bay to swim and snorkel, and usually feed you along the way. Smaller sailboats keep numbers low, often around ten people, so it feels less like a factory outing. Prices for a group boat trip start from roughly €25 and climb with the size and length of the tour.

The other draw is wildlife. The channel between Tenerife and La Gomera is home to resident pods of pilot whales and bottlenose dolphins all year round, and most cliff cruises detour to look for them on the way. Sightings are common but never guaranteed, so treat them as a bonus rather than the whole point. If dolphins are the priority, choose a trip that specifically markets whale and dolphin watching, and go earlier in the day when the sea is calmer.

Getting on the water your own way

If a big catamaran is not your thing, there are more active options. Guided sea kayak and snorkel trips paddle out from the harbour and hug the coast beneath the cliffs, stopping in clear water to snorkel, and they typically run around €45 to €65 for a couple of hours with gear included. It is a lovely way to feel the scale of the place at sea level, and no experience is needed on the calmer days.

For a faster thrill, jet ski safaris head out along the same coastline with a guide, usually in the region of €60 to €120 depending on how long you go and whether you ride single or double. Whatever you pick, book the day before and check the forecast. The west coast can get choppy, and operators will cancel or shorten trips when the swell is up, which is the right call even if it is disappointing.

Masca, the village

A twenty-minute drive up from the coast, on a road that switches back and forth across the mountains, sits Masca. It is one of those places that turns up on every Tenerife postcard: a scatter of stone houses along a knife-edge ridge, palm trees, terraced gardens, and a gorge falling away on either side. For years it was almost cut off, and it still feels remote in a way little else on the island does.

You can visit Masca purely for the village and the views, and plenty of people do. There are a couple of small restaurants and viewpoints, and the drive itself is half the experience, though it is not one for nervous passengers. Parking is limited, so go early or late in the day. The road is narrow and busy at midday.

The Masca gorge hike

Below the village, the barranco de Masca cuts a deep, dramatic gorge down to the sea, and walking it is the headline adventure of the west coast. It is a one-way descent of roughly five kilometres, steep and rocky, ending at a hidden cove where you catch a boat out. The scenery is extraordinary: towering rock walls, pools, and a real sense of being somewhere wild.

Since reopening, the trail is tightly managed. You book a timed slot online in advance, and the ticket now bundles the trail permit with a shuttle, a helmet, and insurance. Expect to pay in the region of €40 per adult, with reduced rates for children and Canary residents, plus a small online booking fee. Access has also changed: private cars are restricted, and you reach the start via the TITSA route 355 bus from Santiago del Teide, arriving at the pickup point at least an hour before your slot. Crucially, you exit the gorge by boat to Los Gigantes, and that boat ticket must be pre-booked. There is no walking back up.

Be honest with yourself about this one. The Masca gorge is a proper mountain hike, not a stroll. It is a steep, uneven descent that takes most people three to four hours, in heat, with loose rock and few places to bail out once you start. It is not suitable for unfit walkers, anyone with knee or balance problems, or small children. You need real walking shoes, water, sun cover, and a head for exposure. If any of that gives you pause, the boat trip along the cliffs gives you the same coastline with none of the risk.

Getting there and making a day of it

From the southern resorts, Los Gigantes is an easy drive of about 45 to 60 minutes up the west coast on the TF-1 and then the coast road. It is one of the more straightforward day trips you can do without a tour. Masca village is another twenty minutes or so up the mountain from there on that winding road.

The natural rhythm of a day here is simple. Drive up in the morning, take a cliff boat trip from the marina, then have a long lunch by the harbour and drive up to Masca for the late-afternoon light before heading home. If you are set on the gorge hike, build the whole day around your booked slot instead, since the bus times and boat exit dictate everything else. Either way, it pairs well with the rest of the island's west, and you can find plenty of other ideas on our other things to do page.

Frequently asked questions

Can you see Los Gigantes without a boat?

Yes. There are good views from the harbour wall and from Playa de los Guios beach in Los Gigantes town. But the cliffs feel far more impressive from the water looking up, which is why almost everyone takes a boat trip.

Are you likely to see dolphins or whales?

Fairly likely. Pilot whales and bottlenose dolphins live in the channel year-round and most cliff cruises look for them. Sightings are common but never guaranteed, so treat them as a happy bonus and go earlier in the day when the sea is calmer.

Do you need to book the Masca gorge hike in advance?

Yes, and well ahead. You reserve a timed slot online, the ticket includes the shuttle, helmet and insurance, and you must separately have a pre-booked boat out at the bottom. There is no turning up on the day, and no walking back up.

How hard is the Masca gorge walk?

Demanding. It is a steep, rocky one-way descent of about five kilometres that takes most people three to four hours in the heat. It is not suitable for unfit walkers, anyone with knee problems, or small children. Proper shoes and water are essential.

How long does it take to get there from the south?

Around 45 to 60 minutes by car from the main southern resorts up to Los Gigantes, then roughly another twenty minutes on a winding mountain road up to Masca village.
