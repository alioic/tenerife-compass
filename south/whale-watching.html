Whale & dolphin watching in Tenerife

There is a pod of pilot whales living in the channel between Tenerife and La Gomera, all year, every day. You do not need luck or a lucky season. You need a decent boat and a couple of hours.

Home› South Tenerife› Things to Do› Whale Watching

Why the sightings are so reliable here

Most whale-watching destinations sell you a maybe. Tenerife sells you a near-certainty. The stretch of Atlantic off the south-west coast is deep, sheltered and rich in squid, and a resident population of around 300 short-finned pilot whales lives there permanently. Bottlenose dolphins are around too, in the same water, year-round. Because the animals do not migrate away, most operators report sightings on the large majority of trips, and many will offer a free repeat sail if you somehow see nothing.

Between roughly April and October the cast grows. Fin whales, sperm whales, Bryde's whales and pods of pilot whales passing through all turn up from time to time, along with the occasional loggerhead turtle. Those are the bonus rounds. The pilot whales and dolphins are the reason the trip works whatever week you visit.

It is worth saying plainly: these are wild animals in open water, not a show. A good crew reads the sea, follows the pods at a respectful distance and lets the animals decide how close they come. Some days a mother and calf drift past the hull for ten minutes; other days you watch dark fins roll fifty metres off. Both are the real thing.

Where the boats leave from

Almost every trip departs from one of two harbours in the south, and both are easy to reach from the resort strip.

Puerto Colón, in Costa Adeje, is the busier of the two and where most catamarans and sailing yachts tie up. It sits right below the Playa de las Américas and Costa Adeje hotels, so for most visitors it is a short taxi or a walk. Signage inside the marina can be confusing, so note your dock number (operators often say "Dock 10" or "Dock 15") when you book.

Los Cristianos harbour, a few minutes further south, tends to have the larger, cheaper big-boat trips and some of the smaller local operators. If you are staying in Los Cristianos itself, starting here saves you a transfer. Wherever you sail from, the same pods live in the same water, so the harbour matters for convenience, not for what you will see.

The three kinds of trip

Prices shift with season and demand, so treat these as a guide rather than a quote. Broadly, tours fall into three tiers.

Budget big-boat

A large motor boat or older catamaran, 40 to 100-plus passengers, 2 to 3 hours. Cheap, cheerful and fine if you just want to see whales without frills. Expect a busier deck and a shorter time on the water.

The one thing to check: the Blue Boat flag

If you take one piece of advice from this page, take this. Before you book, check that the boat carries the Barco Azul ("Blue Boat") flag: a yellow flag with a blue logo, granted by the Canary Islands authorities to vessels that follow the whale-watching rules.

The rules exist because the resident pilot whales are protected, and careless boats stress them. A Blue Boat has to keep to a low speed near the animals, approach slowly and never head straight at a pod or cut across its path. Numbers of boats around any group are limited, and there are fixed distances they must keep. Choosing a certified operator is not just an ethics badge; it usually means a calmer, better-run trip with a crew who know the animals.

Quick check: ask the operator directly whether they hold the Barco Azul licence, or look for the yellow-and-blue flag in their photos. If a listing is oddly cheap and coy about it, assume it is not certified and book elsewhere.

What you will realistically see, and when to go

Set your expectations at pilot whales and bottlenose dolphins, and treat anything larger as a happy surprise. Pilot whales are dark, blunt-headed and unhurried; you usually see backs and fins rolling at the surface rather than dramatic breaching. Dolphins are livelier and will sometimes ride the bow wave. Bring patience and you will be rewarded more often than not.

The sea off the south-west is sheltered and calm most mornings, which is why the industry is based here. For the flattest water and the best light, go on an early or mid-morning departure; wind and chop tend to build through the afternoon. Sightings are good all year, so pick your date around comfort rather than season. If you are prone to seasickness, a larger, heavier boat rides steadier than a small yacht.

Small print that saves the day

  • Seasickness: take a tablet 45–60 minutes before boarding, not on the boat. Stay on deck, look at the horizon, skip the big breakfast.
  • Bring: sunscreen and a hat (the Atlantic sun is stronger than it feels with a breeze), a light layer, sunglasses, and a towel and swimsuit if your trip has a swim stop.
  • Cameras: a phone is fine, but keep the strap on. Do not lean out chasing the shot.
  • Timing: arrive 20–30 minutes early to find your dock and check in.

Common questions

Will we actually see whales?

Almost certainly. The pilot whales and dolphins live in this water permanently rather than migrating through, so sightings happen on the large majority of trips. Many operators offer a free repeat sail if you see nothing at all, which is rare.

When is the best time of year to go?

Any time. The resident pods are here all twelve months, so there is no closed season. April to October adds a chance of larger passing species like fin and sperm whales, but the core experience is the same year-round. Pick your date for comfort, and favour a morning departure for calmer sea.

How much does a whale-watching trip cost?

Roughly from ~€22–35 for a budget big-boat, ~€40–60 for a mid-range catamaran with a swim stop and refreshments, and ~€55–75 for a premium small-group or eco sailing trip. Prices vary with season and what is included, so check current availability before booking.

What is the Barco Azul flag and why does it matter?

Barco Azul, or Blue Boat, is an official Canary Islands certification for vessels that follow the whale-watching regulations: low speeds near the animals, careful approaches and limits on how many boats crowd a pod. Choosing a certified boat protects the whales and usually means a better-run trip. Look for the yellow flag with the blue logo, or ask the operator directly.

Where do the boats leave from?

Mostly Puerto Colón in Costa Adeje and Los Cristianos harbour, both in the south. Puerto Colón has more catamarans and sailing yachts; Los Cristianos has more of the larger budget boats. The same pods live off both, so choose whichever is closest to where you are staying.
