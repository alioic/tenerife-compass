South Tenerife

Where to eat and drink in South Tenerife

Fresh fish landed that morning in La Caleta, garlicky papas arrugadas at a roadside guachinche, and eight of the island's nine Michelin stars within a short drive. Here is where to eat well at every budget, plus the honest word on bars and nightlife.

Start here

The lay of the land

Each corner of the south has its own food personality. Knowing which is which saves you a lot of wandering.

The south is smaller than it looks on a map, so you can eat cheaply one night and splurge the next without moving hotels. A quick orientation:

  • La Caleta — seafood over the water, refined and relatively quiet. Best at lunch.
  • Costa Adeje, El Duque & Fañabé — upscale beachfront dining and rooftop cocktails.
  • Los Cristianos — a working harbour with traditional fish restaurants and honest prices.
  • Las Américas — the full range, from family tribute-act pubs to the loud late-night Verónicas strip.
  • Adeje town and inland — guachinches and Canarian classics, away from the resorts.

One rule that pays for itself all week: the closer to the sand, the higher the bill. Walk a street or two inland and the same plate often drops 20 to 30 percent.

Eat like a local

Local Canarian food

Canarian cooking is simple, salty and built around what the islands grow and catch. This is the food to seek out at guachinches and family restaurants inland, where prices are lowest and the cooking is most honest.

If you try nothing else, start here:

  • Papas arrugadas con mojo — salt-wrinkled little potatoes with red (mojo rojo) and green (mojo verde) sauces. The island's signature side.
  • Fresh grilled fish — vieja, cherne and sama are the local names to look for, usually grilled whole and priced by weight.
  • Lapas — grilled limpets with garlic and mojo verde, straight off the rocks.
  • Carne de cabra — slow-cooked goat, rich and tender. Also look for conejo en salmorejo (marinated rabbit).
  • Ropa vieja — shredded meat and chickpea stew, and carne fiesta, marinated fried pork chunks.
  • Gofio & escaldón — toasted grain flour, worked into everything from breakfast to escaldón, a thick fish-broth porridge.
  • Queso asado con mojo — grilled local goat's cheese with mojo. A perfect starter.
  • Bienmesabe, frangollo & quesillo — the sweet finish: an almond cream, a milk-and-lemon pudding and a firm island flan.

To drink, keep it Canarian. The volcanic wines are the real find here.

  • Listán Negro — the local red, light and a little smoky.
  • Listán Blanco — a crisp white that goes with everything from the sea.
  • Malvasía — aromatic and often off-dry, much of it from DO Abona in the south.
  • Dorada & Tropical — the two island lagers, best ice-cold in a caña.
  • Ron miel — honey rum, sweet and warming, usually an after-dinner pour.
  • Barraquito — the island's layered coffee: espresso, condensed milk, Licor 43 and a twist of lemon. Order one at least once.

Bars, sunset spots & nightlife

The south covers everything from a quiet glass of Malvasía at sunset to clubs going until dawn. Here is the honest version so you can pick the right corner for your night.

The loud end. The Verónicas strip in Las Américas is the island's big late-night zone: bars and clubs running from around 3am to 6am, young crowds and cheap drinks. It's fun if that's your scene, but keep a close eye on your belongings and expect plenty of promoters. Nearby, Starco is a similar cluster of bars.

The relaxed end. The Patch is the long-running spot for over-25s, with tribute acts and a friendlier, less frantic feel. Along the coast, the beach clubs Papagayo and Monkey Beach do daytime-into-evening music and cocktails on the sand.

For a nicer drink. If you want a view rather than a dancefloor:

  • Zambra Sky Bar — a Costa Adeje rooftop with a glass-bottom pool. The showpiece sundowner spot.
  • Maresía Atlantic Bar — a rooftop over La Caleta, calmer and right on the water.
  • The 16th — the Hard Rock Hotel rooftop, slick cocktails and a party edge.
  • Bar Fresh — the ship-shaped bar in Costa Adeje, known for its Sunset Selection cocktails.

Sunset and family evenings. La Caleta is the pick for a relaxed sundowner, with low-key bars along the seafront. For an easy family evening, stroll the Fañabé and El Duque promenades, where the pace is gentle and the terraces are made for a slow ice cream after dinner.

Good to know

Food & drink FAQ

Where is the best seafood in South Tenerife?

La Caleta is the classic choice, a small village of restaurants right on the rocks where the fish comes straight from the boats. La Vieja and La Masía del Mar are long-standing favourites. For the same idea with lower prices, head to the Los Cristianos harbour and try El Cine or Abordo. Seafood is best at lunch, when the catch is freshest.

Which Canarian dishes should I try first?

Start with papas arrugadas con mojo (salt-wrinkled potatoes with red and green sauces), fresh grilled local fish such as vieja or cherne, and queso asado con mojo to begin. If you eat meat, carne de cabra (goat) and ropa vieja are the classics. Finish with a barraquito coffee. Guachinches inland are the cheapest and most authentic place to eat all of this.

Do I need to book Michelin restaurants in advance?

Yes, especially the two-star tables, El Rincón de Juan Carlos and M.B. by Martín Berasategui. These book up weeks ahead in busy seasons. Reserve as soon as your dates are fixed. The one-star restaurants are a little easier, but the south holds eight of the island's nine stars, so demand is high year-round.

How can I eat well without paying resort prices?

Walk one or two streets back from the seafront, where the same meal often costs 20 to 30 percent less. Look for the menu del día at lunch, usually two or three courses with a drink for around €10 to €15. Order the local wines (Listán and Malvasía) rather than imports, and stick to Dorada or Tropical on draught.
