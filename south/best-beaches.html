South Tenerife

The best beaches in South Tenerife

The south is where the island keeps its sunniest, most swimmable stretches of sand. Some are polished and gold, raked every morning in front of five-star hotels; others are raw, windswept and wonderful. Here is an honest run-through of which beach suits which kind of day.

South Tenerife's best beaches, one by one

One thing worth knowing first: most of the south's golden beaches are not natural. The sand was imported and the bays were built with breakwaters, which is exactly why the water is so calm and the sand so pale. The wilder beaches keep their darker volcanic grit and their Atlantic swell. Both have their place, and below we work west to east through the ones actually worth your towel.

Playa del Duque
Blue Flag

Playa del Duque

The south's most upscale beach, fronting the smart hotels of Costa Adeje. Soft golden sand, calm sheltered water and a row of chic beach clubs with stripy loungers. It is small and gets busy, but the swimming is gentle and the people-watching is half the point.

Free · sunbeds ~€15–20

Playa Fañabé
Family favourite

Playa Fañabé

A long Blue Flag beach that runs into Playa Torviscas, backed by one of the liveliest promenades in the south. Wide golden sand, gentle water and every facility you could want, from pedalos to ice cream. The obvious pick if you are travelling with kids and want everything to hand.

Free · sunbeds ~€10–15

Playa de las Vistas
Blue Flag

Playa de las Vistas

The wide, pale crescent between Los Cristianos and Las Américas, and the most swimmer-friendly beach in the south. Imported golden sand, a breakwater that keeps the water calm and flat, plus a fountain offshore and excellent accessibility, including beach wheelchairs. Great for weaker swimmers and small children.

Free · sunbeds ~€10–15

Playa del Camisón
Family favourite

Playa del Camisón

A small, tidy, sheltered bay just south of Las Américas, protected by rock breakwaters that make the water almost pool-calm. Golden sand, tucked-away feel and green lawns behind it. Quieter than its big neighbours and a lovely spot for families who find Las Vistas too hectic.

Free · sunbeds ~€10–15

Playa de Troya
Lively

Playa de Troya

Right in the heart of Playa de las Américas, this is the busy, buzzy town beach. Golden sand, decent swimming and a wall of bars, shops and restaurants a few steps behind you. Come for the atmosphere and the convenience rather than peace and quiet.

Free · sunbeds ~€10–15

Playa El Bobo / La Pinta
Good swimming

Playa El Bobo / La Pinta

A pair of smaller sandy coves down by Puerto Colón marina in Torviscas. Less polished than the flagship beaches, but the sheltered water here is some of the clearest and best for swimming and snorkelling, and it is the launch point for most boat trips.

Free · sunbeds ~€10–15

La Tejita
Wild & natural

La Tejita

The antidote to the resort beaches. A long, open, natural beach near El Médano, sitting beneath the red cinder cone of Montaña Roja. Darker natural sand, more wind and real Atlantic waves, and a part-naturist far end. Undeveloped and beautiful, but bring your own shade and water.

Free · no sunbeds, few facilities

Playa de los Cristianos
Blue Flag

Playa de los Cristianos

The sheltered town beach beside the old fishing harbour, calmer and more low-key than Las Vistas next door. Handy golden sand for a quick swim between other plans, with the town's shops and seafood restaurants right there. Watch for ferry traffic at the harbour end.

Free · sunbeds ~€10–15

Read the sand and the flags

Two quick things to remember. First, the pale gold sand on the resort beaches was imported; the darker, coarser sand you meet elsewhere is the island's natural volcanic grit, and it holds the heat, so flip-flops help by midday. Second, this is the open Atlantic. The built-up beaches with breakwaters stay calm, but exposed beaches like La Tejita and anywhere on the windier southeast can have real waves and currents. Always check the lifeguard flag, and if it is red, stay out.

Making a beach day of it

The beauty of the south is how close everything sits. A near-continuous seafront promenade links Los Cristianos, Las Américas and Costa Adeje, so you can hop between Las Vistas, Troya, Fañabé and El Duque on foot and pick whichever suits your mood. Mornings are calmest and least crowded; by early afternoon the flagship beaches fill up and the sunbeds go fast.

Choosing a base around the beach you like most makes the whole trip easier, so it is worth reading our guide to where to stay before you book. And when you have had enough sand for one day, there is plenty more to fill an itinerary in things to do, from whale watching out of Puerto Colón to a day up on Mount Teide.

Which is the best beach in South Tenerife?

For all-round appeal, Playa de las Vistas in Los Cristianos is hard to beat: it is wide, has calm, shallow water thanks to its breakwater, imported golden sand and excellent accessibility. For a more upscale feel choose Playa del Duque, and for wild, natural scenery head to La Tejita.

Are the beaches in South Tenerife sandy or rocky?

Most of the main resort beaches are sandy, but much of that gold sand was imported and the bays were built with breakwaters. Away from the resorts you will find the island's natural darker, coarser volcanic sand, as at La Tejita. There are rocky sections too, so water shoes are handy for swimming off the quieter spots.

Are South Tenerife beaches good for swimming?

The built-up beaches with breakwaters, like Las Vistas, Fañabé and Camisón, are calm and well suited to families and weaker swimmers. Exposed beaches such as La Tejita face the open Atlantic and can have waves and currents. Always check the lifeguard flag before you go in and never swim on a red flag.

How much do sunbeds and umbrellas cost?

On the main beaches expect roughly €10–15 for a sunbed, a little more at the smarter beach clubs on Playa del Duque. The beaches themselves are free, and wilder beaches like La Tejita have few or no facilities, so bring your own shade and water.

Is La Tejita a naturist beach?

It is part-naturist. The far end towards Montaña Roja is traditionally used by naturists, while the rest of this long natural beach is mixed. It is more exposed and windier than the resort beaches, which makes it popular with kitesurfers and anyone after a quieter, more natural feel.
