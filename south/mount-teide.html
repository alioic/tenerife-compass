Mount Teide from the south

It sits in the middle of the island, snow on top some winters, and you can be standing at 3,555 metres barely an hour after leaving your sun lounger in Costa Adeje. Here is how to actually do it.

Home› South Tenerife› Things to Do› Mount Teide

What Teide actually is

Mount Teide is a volcano, and at 3,715 metres it is the highest peak in Spain and the third-tallest volcano on Earth measured from its base on the ocean floor. The whole national park around it is a UNESCO World Heritage Site, a lunar-looking bowl of lava fields, twisted rock and pale sand that film crews use when they want somewhere that does not look like Earth.

The skies here are Starlight-certified, which is a formal designation given to places with genuinely dark, clear nights. That is why one of the world's major observatories sits on the neighbouring ridge, and why a lot of people come up not for the daytime views but for the stars. From the south of the island it is the single most rewarding day (or evening) trip you can make away from the coast.

Getting there from the south

You have three realistic options, and which one suits you depends mostly on whether you want to drive mountain roads and how much freedom you want once you are up there.

Self-drive is the most flexible. From Costa Adeje, Los Cristianos or Playa de las Américas it is roughly an hour to an hour and a half up, depending on which road you take and how often you stop for photos. The TF-21 via Vilaflor is the classic route, climbing through pine forest before the trees give way to lava. The roads are well surfaced but they are winding, so if anyone in the car gets travel sick, sit them in the front. Fuel up before you leave the coast, and start early to beat both the tour buses and the afternoon cloud that often rolls in below the summit.

Public bus is possible but needs planning. TITSA Line 342 runs from Costa Adeje up into the park, and here is the catch that trips people up: there is only one departure up and one departure back per day. At the time of writing it leaves Costa Adeje around 09:25 and starts back down from the Teide area around 15:15. Miss the return and you are stuck, so treat those times as fixed and check the current TITSA timetable before you go. It is cheap and scenic, but it gives you no margin for error.

An organised guided tour takes the driving and the logistics off your plate. A guide handles pickup from your hotel, tells you what you are looking at, and often includes the cable car. It is the easy button, and for a first visit it is a perfectly sensible one.

The cable car

The Teide cable car (teleférico) carries you from the base station at 2,356 metres up to 3,555 metres in about eight minutes. That upper station is not the true summit, but it is high enough for enormous views over the crater and, on a clear day, across to the neighbouring islands of La Gomera, La Palma and El Hierro.

A round-trip ticket for a non-resident adult is around €40, with children roughly half that. An ascent-only or descent-only ticket is cheaper if you plan to hike one direction, though the walking trails at the top need care and, in one case, a permit (more on that below). Book online in advance. Time slots are limited and the on-the-day queue at the base station can be long, especially mid-morning when the tour buses arrive. Booking ahead locks in your slot and usually the price.

One honest warning: the cable car closes in high wind, and it does so fairly often. If it is shut when you arrive there is no appeal and no alternative to the top. Have a plan B for the day, and if the summit is your whole reason for going, keep an eye on the forecast.

The free summit permit

The cable car drops you at 3,555 metres. To walk the final stretch up the summit cone to the true peak at 3,715 metres you need a free access permit, and it is completely separate from your cable car ticket.

You book it in advance through the official national parks reservation system (reservasparquesnacionales.es). It costs nothing, but that is exactly why it goes fast: only a limited number of people are let onto the cone in each two-hour time slot, and popular dates disappear weeks, sometimes months, ahead. Book as early as you can, ideally as soon as you know your travel dates. A ranger checks your permit and your ID at the start of the trail, so bring the confirmation and your passport. No permit, no summit, no exceptions. If you do not have one, you can still walk the two shorter marked trails from the upper station, which are free and still spectacular.

Stargazing and sunset tours

If you only do one organised thing up here, consider the evening rather than the day. Because the air is thin, dry and far from city light, Teide is one of the best places in Europe to look at the night sky, and the difference between what you see here and what you see from a lit resort is not subtle.

A typical sunset-and-stargazing tour runs somewhere between €60 and €110 per person depending on whether dinner and drinks are included. You watch the sun go down over the lava, then a guide sets up telescopes and walks you through what you are looking at while your eyes adjust. Bring far more warm clothing than feels reasonable on the coast, because once the sun is gone the temperature drops hard. A guided tour also solves the driving-down-a-mountain-in-the-dark problem, which is worth something on its own.

What to wear and expect

The number one mistake is dressing for the beach. It can be 25°C on the coast and close to freezing at the top, with a wind that makes it feel colder. Bring a proper jacket, long trousers and closed shoes, even in summer. In winter there is often snow, and the access roads can close entirely for a day or two after a storm.

You will also feel the altitude. At 3,555 metres the air holds far less oxygen, so move slowly, don't be surprised if you get a little breathless or light-headed, and give yourself time. The national parks authority advises people with heart or serious respiratory conditions, and pregnant women, to skip the summit cone. Bring water, sunglasses and sun cream: the UV up here is fierce even when it feels cold.

What to see up there

Even without setting foot in the cable car, the park itself is the attraction. Roques de García is the most photographed spot, a cluster of eroded rock formations with an easy circular walking trail beneath them and a big view across the Ucanca plain. The Pico Viejo lookout, reached on foot from the upper cable car station, stares straight down into a huge secondary crater. And the drive across Las Cañadas, the vast caldera floor, is worth slowing right down for. Give yourself a few unhurried stops rather than racing between the headline sights.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need a permit to go up in the cable car?

No. The cable car is a paid ticket you book separately, and it takes you to 3,555 metres with no permit needed. The free permit is only required for the final walk to the 3,715 m summit.

How far ahead should I book the summit permit?

As far ahead as you can. Slots are free and therefore in high demand, and busy dates go weeks to months in advance through reservasparquesnacionales.es. Book the moment you know your dates.

Can I get to Teide by public bus from the south?

Yes, on TITSA Line 342 from Costa Adeje, but there is only one bus up and one back each day (roughly 09:25 up, 15:15 down). Check the current timetable and do not miss the return.

Is the cable car ever closed?

Fairly often, yes. It closes in high wind, and the whole area can be affected by snow and road closures in winter. Check the forecast and have a backup plan for the day.

Do I need to worry about the altitude?

Most people are fine but feel a bit breathless. Move slowly and drink water. The park authority recommends that people with heart or respiratory conditions, and pregnant women, avoid the summit cone.
