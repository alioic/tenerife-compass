Where to stay in Tenerife

Tenerife is really several holidays sharing one island. Pick the wrong coast and you can spend a week under cloud while the other side bakes. Here is how the regions actually differ.

Home› Where to Stay
Start here

The one thing that decides it

Trade winds hit the north of the island and pile cloud against the mountains. The south sits in the rain shadow. That single fact explains almost every difference between the regions.

If you want reliable poolside sun, the south-west is the answer, and it is where the resort industry has been built. If you want green landscapes, historic towns, better-value food and do not mind grey mornings, the north is far more interesting and noticeably cheaper. The capital and La Laguna are for people who want a real city rather than a resort, and the east is for wind sports and a slower, more local pace.

Most first-time visitors stay in the south and take day trips everywhere else, which works well. The island is small: you can drive from Costa Adeje to Puerto de la Cruz in about an hour and a quarter.

El Médano is a low-rise beach town wrapped around a sandy bay, one of Europe's best-known windsurf and kitesurf spots, ten minutes from the south airport. Wonderful if you are here for the wind, less so if you want a still day on a towel.

Check the walk to the beach

"Costa Adeje" covers a lot of ground, from seafront hotels to properties a steep fifteen minutes uphill. The area name in a listing tells you very little, so always check the actual distance on a map before you book.

When to book

Timing and prices

May, June, September and October are the sweet spot: the weather is still excellent, the sea is at its warmest in early autumn, and rooms cost noticeably less. July and August are the hottest and busiest, and Christmas, New Year and Carnival in February sell out well in advance.

Winter is a second high season here, because northern Europeans come for the sun rather than in spite of it, so December to February is not the bargain it would be elsewhere in Europe. The five-star cluster around El Duque and Fañabé and the best adults-only hotels fill first in every peak period.

All-inclusive dominates the family resort market in Costa Adeje and makes budgeting easy if you plan to stay put. Self-catering is usually the smarter choice in Los Cristianos, Las Américas and the northern towns, where dozens of restaurants are within a short walk and you would be paying for meals you never eat.

Good to know

Where to stay FAQ

What is the best area to stay in Tenerife?

For a first visit and for reliable sun, the south-west: Costa Adeje for comfort and polish, Playa de las Américas for nightlife and a central base, Los Cristianos for value and a real town feel. If you care more about scenery, food and history than poolside weather, the north around Puerto de la Cruz and La Orotava is more rewarding and cheaper.

Should I stay in the north or the south of Tenerife?

They are different holidays. The south is sunnier and drier almost every day of the year, which is why the resorts are there. The north is greener, cooler and cloudier, with historic towns, gardens and better-value restaurants, but the Atlantic is rough and lidos replace safe swimming beaches. Many people base in the south and day-trip north.

Is Tenerife expensive?

It is one of the better-value warm-winter destinations in Europe. Expect roughly €10 to €15 for a three-course menu del día, €2 to €3 for a beer away from the seafront, and mid-range double rooms from about €100 a night in shoulder season. The Canaries also have a lower sales tax than mainland Spain.

Do I need a hire car?

Not for a resort holiday in the south, where everything is walkable and buses and taxis are cheap. A car pays for itself if you want to reach Teide, Anaga, Masca or the quieter western villages on your own schedule. See our getting around guide.

Which part of Tenerife has the best weather?

The south-west, comfortably. It gets around 2,800 hours of sun a year and only about 115 mm of rain, most of it on a handful of winter days. The trade winds drop their moisture on the northern slopes, which is what makes that side green and this side dry.
