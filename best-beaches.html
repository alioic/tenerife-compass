Island guide

The best beaches in Tenerife

This is an island where the sand changes colour depending which coast you stand on: pale gold and pool-calm in the south, jet black and loud with surf in the north and west. Picking the right coast for the day you want matters more than picking the famous beach.

Home› Best beaches

How Tenerife's coastline works

Four coasts, four completely different beach days.

The south and south-west are the sheltered, sunny side. This is where the wide golden resort beaches are, most protected by breakwaters and several built with imported sand. If you want to swim without thinking about it, go here.

The north and west are volcanic: grey to jet-black sand and an Atlantic that arrives with real swell. Swimming often depends on the flag rather than your mood. In Puerto de la Cruz the answer to that rough sea was to build seawater lidos and use natural rock pools, and they are part of the appeal rather than a consolation prize.

The north-east is the surprise: one long golden crescent at Las Teresitas outside Santa Cruz and, a winding half hour further, the wild black beaches of Anaga. The east coast around El Médano is windy and barely developed, which is why the windsurfers live there.

Respect the flags

This is the open Atlantic, not the Mediterranean. The sheltered southern bays stay flat most of the year, but the north and west coasts have genuine swell and currents, and people get into trouble every season. Check the lifeguard flag, and if it is red, do not swim, however calm one patch of water looks. Where there is no lifeguard, be more conservative, not less.

The south: golden sand and calm water

The most reliable swimming on the island, and the busiest sand. Sunbeds run roughly €10–15.

Playa de las Vistas
Blue Flag

Playa de las Vistas

Wide imported golden sand between Los Cristianos and Las Américas, with a breakwater that keeps the water flat and the best accessibility on the island. The safest family swimming in Tenerife, and it knows it, so come early.

Free · sunbeds ~€10–15 · South

Playa del Duque
Blue Flag

Playa del Duque

The most upscale beach in the south, fronting the smart end of Costa Adeje: soft golden sand, sheltered water and a row of chic beach clubs. Small, busy, and not cheap once you sit down.

Free · sunbeds ~€15–20 · South

Playa Fañabé
Family favourite

Playa Fañabé

Two kilometres of Blue Flag sand backed by one of the liveliest promenades in Costa Adeje. The obvious pick with kids, since gentle water, shade, toilets and ice cream are all within a minute's walk.

Free · sunbeds ~€10–15 · South

Playa del Camisón
Family favourite

Playa del Camisón

A small sheltered cove just along from Las Américas, tucked behind rock breakwaters that leave the water almost pool-calm. The answer if Las Vistas feels too hectic.

Free · sunbeds ~€10–15 · South

Playa de Troya
Lively

Playa de Troya

The town beach in the middle of Playa de las Américas, with bars and shops a few steps behind the sand. Come for the convenience and the noise, not for quiet.

Free · sunbeds ~€10–15 · South

El Bobo / La Pinta
Good swimming

Playa El Bobo / La Pinta

Two smaller coves by Puerto Colón in Torviscas. Less polished than the flagship beaches, but the sheltered water is clear and good for a proper swim.

Free · sunbeds ~€10–15 · South

Los Cristianos
Blue Flag

Los Cristianos town beach

The sheltered strip beside the old harbour, calmer and lower-key than Las Vistas next door. Handy for a swim between other plans, with ferry traffic at the far end.

Free · sunbeds ~€10–15 · South

There is more detail on all of these, including which one to base yourself near, in the full south beaches guide.

The west: black sand under the cliffs

Dark volcanic sand, big backdrops and a sea that is not always in the mood.

Playa de la Arena
Blue Flag

Playa de la Arena

A compact bay of jet-black volcanic sand in Puerto de Santiago, well serviced and Blue Flag for over 25 years. The swell here is real, so take the red flag seriously when it flies.

Free · sunbeds ~€10–15 · West

Playa de los Guíos
Cliff views

Playa de los Guíos

A small black-sand beach beside the marina at Los Gigantes, directly under the cliffs. The setting is the reason to come; the beach itself is short and fills up fast.

Free · limited facilities · West

Playa San Juan
Blue Flag

Playa San Juan

A sheltered dark-sand Blue Flag beach in a town that still works as a fishing port. The calmest swimming on this coast, and a rare spot on the west side where the water is usually easy.

Free · sunbeds ~€10–15 · West

Alcalá
Coves

Alcalá

Small coves and natural rock pools rather than one big beach, in a quiet town between San Juan and Playa de la Arena. Better for a slow afternoon than a full beach day.

Free · few facilities · West

The north: volcanic beaches, gardens and sea pools

Greener, cloudier and rougher. The north's answer to a wild Atlantic was to build pools beside it.

Playa Jardín
Black sand

Playa Jardín

Puerto de la Cruz's best beach: black sand backed by lush gardens landscaped by César Manrique. Lovely to look at and to walk, but the sea turns quickly, so watch the flags before committing.

Free · sunbeds available · North

Playa El Socorro
Surf

Playa El Socorro

The surf beach of Los Realejos and one of the better sunset spots on the north coast, lifeguarded in season. The waves are the point here, which makes it a poor choice for casual family swimming.

Free · lifeguards in season · North

El Caletón
Rock pools

El Caletón, Garachico

Natural lava pools formed when the 1706 eruption poured into the sea. Swimmable on calm days, roughly May to October, and closed in swell. The most memorable place to get in the water up north.

Free · calm days only · North

Lago Martiánez
Seawater lido

Lago Martiánez

Not a beach, but the dependable option in Puerto de la Cruz: a seawater lido designed by César Manrique, which exists precisely because the Atlantic here is too rough to rely on. Worth it on a red-flag day.

~€5.50 · entry · North

The north-east: Las Teresitas and wild Anaga

The prettiest big beach on the island, and half an hour away, the rawest ones.

Las Teresitas
Golden sand

Playa de las Teresitas

A 1.3 km crescent of golden Saharan sand at San Andrés, imported in the early 1970s and protected by a breakwater, so the water stays calm. Palms, free parking, fish restaurants in the village, and still refreshingly un-resort-like.

Free · free parking · Santa Cruz

Playa de Benijo
Wild

Playa de Benijo

Wild black sand in Anaga with jagged rock stacks offshore, reached by a stepped path, and arguably the most photogenic beach in Tenerife. The surf is strong and nobody is watching, so swim with great caution or not at all.

Free · no facilities · Anaga

Almáciga & Roque
Wild

Almáciga & Roque de las Bodegas

Benijo's neighbours on the same rugged coast, with the same dark sand and serious water. Roque de las Bodegas is the easier one to reach and has a few well-known fish restaurants behind it.

Free · minimal facilities · Anaga

The east: wind, space and no pretence

Natural sand, constant breeze and the island's water sports capital.

La Tejita
Wild & natural

La Tejita

About a kilometre of open golden-grey sand under the red cone of Montaña Roja, with minimal facilities and room to spread out. There is a long-established naturist section at the eastern end. Bring your own shade and water.

Free · few facilities · East

Playa El Médano
Windsurf

Playa El Médano

A sandy town bay usually full of windsurf sails, and a good deal more relaxed than the big southern resorts. Cafés and board hire are right there. Expect wind: that is the character of the place.

Free · town facilities · East

El Cabezo
Kitesurf

El Cabezo

The kitesurf beach on the far side of El Médano, great to watch and the obvious base if you are here to ride. Not a swimming or lounging beach.

Free · board hire nearby · East

Practical things worth knowing

Costs. Every beach in Tenerife is free to walk onto; what you pay for is a sunbed and parasol, usually €10–15 a day on the main southern and western beaches and more at the beach clubs on Playa del Duque. Wild beaches like Benijo and La Tejita have no hire at all. Lago Martiánez charges around €5.50 to get in.

What Blue Flag means. It is an award for water quality, safety provision, facilities and environmental management, not a verdict on how pretty a beach is. Several superb beaches here have no flag simply because they have nothing to certify.

Imported versus volcanic sand. Tenerife is a volcano, so its natural sand is dark grey to black. The pale gold at Las Vistas and Las Teresitas was brought in, and those bays were shaped with breakwaters, which is exactly why they are calm. Black sand holds heat and gets painful underfoot by midday, so keep flip-flops within reach.

Sea temperature and sun. The water sits at roughly 19°C in late winter and climbs to about 24°C by early autumn, so swimming works year-round, though February and March feel bracing. UV is high all year, including on the cloudy north-coast days that feel deceptively mild.

Parking. Las Teresitas has free parking that fills by mid-morning at weekends, and Anaga beaches have very little roadside space on narrow approach roads, so arrive early or take the bus. In the southern resorts parking near the promenade is limited and paid.

Start with the full south beaches guide if you are staying in Costa Adeje, Las Américas or Los Cristianos, since that is where most of the calm swimming is. For the volcanic side and the sea pools, see North Tenerife. Las Teresitas and the road into Anaga are covered in Santa Cruz, the windy natural coast around El Médano in East Tenerife, and the cliffs and fishing towns in West Tenerife.

Which is the best beach in Tenerife?

For looks, Playa de las Teresitas near Santa Cruz: a 1.3 km golden crescent with palms, calm protected water and no resort strip behind it. For dependable family swimming, Playa de las Vistas in Los Cristianos is the better choice. For drama, nothing beats Benijo in Anaga, though you should not count on swimming there.

Is the sand in Tenerife real or imported?

Both. Tenerife's natural sand is volcanic, from dark grey to jet black, as at Playa de la Arena and Playa Jardín. The pale golden sand at Playa de las Vistas and Playa de las Teresitas was imported, and those bays were built with breakwaters, which is why the water in them is so calm.

Can you swim safely in the north of Tenerife?

Sometimes, and you must check first. The north faces the open Atlantic with genuine swell, so Playa Jardín and El Socorro can be fine one day and off-limits the next. Never swim on a red flag. The reliable alternatives are the lava pools at El Caletón in Garachico on calm days, roughly May to October, and the Lago Martiánez lido in Puerto de la Cruz.

Are Tenerife beaches free?

Yes. All beaches in Tenerife are public and free to use. You only pay for extras: a sunbed and parasol run about €10–15 a day on the main beaches, more at the beach clubs on Playa del Duque. Entry to the Lago Martiánez lido is around €5.50.

Which beach is best for families in Tenerife?

Playa de las Vistas in Los Cristianos, thanks to its breakwater, calm shallow water and excellent accessibility. Playa Fañabé in Costa Adeje is the close runner-up for facilities, and Playa del Camisón is a good quieter option. Las Teresitas works well too if you are near Santa Cruz.
