Windsurfing & kitesurfing in El Médano

Steady trade winds, a wide sandy bay and schools that teach every day of the year. If you have ever wanted to learn a wind sport, this modest little town is one of the easiest places in Europe to do it.

Why here

Why El Médano is Europe's most reliable wind town

The recipe is simple and almost nowhere else has it. The northeast trade winds funnel down the east coast of Tenerife and accelerate around the corner at El Médano, arriving side-onshore over a broad, sandy, gently shelving bay. That combination — dependable wind, sand underfoot, and water you can stand in for the first hundred metres — is exactly what learning a wind sport requires, and it is why the town has been a windsurfing name since the 1980s. The PWA World Cup ran here for years, world champions have come out of this bay, and on a good summer afternoon you can count fifty kites in the air without trying.

What keeps people coming back, though, is that it works in winter too. The wind is less constant from October to December, but the water never drops much below 19°C, the schools stay open, and a January lesson in a light wetsuit beats a July lesson almost anywhere on mainland Europe. There are very few places where "year-round wind sport destination" is not marketing exaggeration. This is one of them.

The spots

Which beach does what

The bay looks like one beach from the boardwalk, but it divides into distinct zones, and the schools take them seriously. Launching a kite in the wrong place will earn you a swift telling-off.

When to come

The wind calendar, honestly

No wind destination delivers every single day, and anyone who tells you El Médano does is selling something. Here is the realistic picture.

The short version

May to September is the reliable season, and July and August are the peak — 20-plus knots on many days, sometimes for weeks on end, which is why the serious riders book their summers here. October to December is noticeably lighter and calmer, but still rideable more days than not, and the schools simply rig bigger kites and sails. January to April sits in between: changeable, with brilliant days and flat ones. There is also a daily rhythm worth knowing — the wind typically builds through late morning as the island heats up and peaks mid-afternoon, so schools stack beginner lessons in the morning and the experts own the bay from about 14:00. If your holiday is specifically a wind trip, come in summer. If the lesson is one item on a broader holiday, any month works; you may just wait a day for conditions.

Learning

Lessons & what they cost

El Médano's schools teach in English, Spanish, German, French and more, year-round, with IKO-affiliated instructors on the kite side. The Duotone Pro Center on the main bay covers the full spread — windsurf, kite, wing foil, even e-foil — while Kitesurf 313 and a handful of other IKO schools work the kite beaches. Prices below are typical starting points; radios-in-helmets, wetsuits and insurance are normally included.

Already ride?

Rental, levels & what to wear

If you can already ride, rental is straightforward but not casual. The kite schools will want to see certification (an IKO card or equivalent) or watch you demonstrate independent riding before handing over gear — the bay is busy and the reef at El Cabezo punishes overconfidence. Windsurf rental is more relaxed, but centres will still size you up honestly and steer you off the biggest days. Buoyancy aids and helmets are standard issue with rented kit; wear them without argument, because everyone from the world-cup riders down does.

On wetsuits: in summer a shorty is plenty, and plenty of people ride in boardshorts on the hottest afternoons. From roughly December to April you will want a 3/2 full suit — the water sits around 19 to 20°C and the wind chill on a long session is real. Schools and rental centres include suits, so this only matters if you are bringing your own.

Beginner honesty

Kitesurfing has a real learning curve, and it is kinder to know that upfront. Expect 6 to 10 hours of instruction before you are riding independently, and do not judge yourself by the two-hour taster — almost nobody rides on day one. Book consecutive days rather than scattered lessons; muscle memory built on Tuesday is half gone by Saturday. Windsurfing is the opposite story: the basics come in an afternoon, and the difficulty arrives later, when you chase planing and harness work. If your holiday only has one free morning, windsurf. If you have three days and some patience, kite.

Off the water

Après-surf, El Médano style

Half the pleasure of learning here is that the town is built around exactly this rhythm. When your arms give out, walk off the sand to Café Flashpoint beside the kite beach — the surf institution where half the customers are still in wetsuits and the terrace looks straight onto El Cabezo's riders. The boardwalk runs from there past the beaches to the plaza, and doing it slowly at the end of the afternoon, watching the last kites against the light, is the town's unofficial closing ceremony. For dinner with more ambition, the tiny La Boquería Taberna does modern tapas worth reserving for. Our full East Tenerife guide has the rest of the eating and drinking picture.

If the trip is genuinely about the wind, stay where the riders stay. Hotel Playa Sur Tenerife has been the windsurfers' hotel for decades, standing alone on the sand between town and Montaña Roja, and KN Arenas del Mar puts you right by the La Jaquita end if kite lessons are the plan. Both are covered, with prices, in the where-to-stay section of the east guide.

Quick answers

Wind sports FAQ

Can a complete beginner learn in a week?

Yes, comfortably, and that is precisely what El Médano is set up for. A three-day beginner kite course of around nine hours gets most people to their first independent rides, leaving spare days for practice or for the wind to cooperate. Windsurfing is faster still — most beginners are sailing back and forth after a single two-hour intro. Book lessons on consecutive days and leave a buffer day in case conditions force a reschedule.

When is it windiest in El Médano?

July and August, when the trade winds blow 20-plus knots on many days and the bay is at its busiest. May, June and September are almost as dependable with fewer crowds. October to December is the calm end of the year — still rideable more often than not, but with lighter wind and bigger kites. The wind also builds through each day, usually peaking mid-afternoon.

Should I learn windsurfing or kitesurfing first?

It depends on your time. Windsurfing gives quick early rewards — basics in an afternoon — then gets harder later. Kitesurfing demands 6 to 10 hours of instruction before independent riding, but progresses faster once you can ride. If you have one free morning, windsurf; if you have three days, take a kite course. There is no obligation to do one before the other, and the schools here teach both from scratch.

Is the water cold?

Not by European standards. The Atlantic around El Médano ranges from roughly 19 to 20°C in late winter to 23 to 24°C in early autumn. In summer a shorty wetsuit or even boardshorts is fine; from December to April a 3/2 full suit keeps long sessions comfortable. Schools and rental centres include wetsuits, so you only need to think about this if you bring your own gear.

Do the schools rent gear, and can anyone rent?

All the main centres rent windsurf, kite and wing kit, but kite rental requires proof of ability — an IKO card or equivalent, or a demonstration that you can ride independently and relaunch your kite. Windsurf rental is more relaxed, though centres will still match the kit to your honest level and the day's conditions. Buoyancy aids and helmets come with rentals and are expected on the water.
